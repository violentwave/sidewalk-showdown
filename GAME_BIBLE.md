## 1. Core Identity

**Sidewalk Showdown** is a raunchy, violent, absurd, 8-bit / 16-bit-inspired arcade fighting game set in a fictional Florida-style fever dream of liquor stores, bus stops, drainage canals, flea markets, laundromats, broken scooters, fake sponsors, weird grudges, trash politics, and local legends who absolutely should not be handed a microphone.

It is an adult comedy/action fighter.

The game is rude, grimy, crude, chaotic, and full of people making terrible decisions with total confidence. Every fighter is exaggerated, flawed, loud, petty, and ridiculous. Nobody is clean. Nobody is normal. Nobody gets out unroasted.

But underneath the broken glass, trash talk, gas station food, bad tattoos, stolen folding chairs, fake livestream charities, and parking-lot beefs, the game has a strange heart.

The playable fighters are not role models. They are disasters. But they are still people with pride, grudges, dignity, dreams, and personal codes that make sense to them, even when they make no sense to anyone else.

The main antagonist, **Tripp Vlogs**, believes these people are nothing more than disposable content. He turns their lives, flaws, fights, and stories into a livestreamed scam.

By the end, the fighters do not become saints. They do not become normal. They do not suddenly fix their lives.

They do something more fitting for this world:

They realize they are being played, turn on the bigger scumbag, wreck his content empire, and take back the story.

Then they immediately start arguing again.

## 2. High-Concept Premise

Washed-up livestreamer **Tripp Vlogs** is desperate.

His channel is dying. His fans are leaving. His sponsors are gone. His latest videos are humiliating failures with titles like:

- “I Ate Gas Station Sushi for 48 Hours”
- “Pranking My Landlord at 3 AM”
- “I Lived Like a Normal Poor for One Day”
- “Public Freakout Challenge Gone Inspirational?”

Then one afternoon, while filming another desperate livestream stunt at a bus stop, Tripp accidentally captures two local sidewalk regulars fighting in the background.

He does not break it up.

He does not call for help.

He zooms in.

The clip goes viral overnight.

For the first time in months, Tripp gets views, comments, followers, reaction videos, memes, outrage, and a sketchy energy drink company in his inbox.

So he does what only a true dirtbag with a ring light would do:

He turns the fight into a tournament.

He announces **Sidewalk Showdown**, a livestreamed street-fighting bracket where strange local fighters compete for a winner-takes-all prize package supposedly including money, fame, housing, redemption, and “a fresh start.”

The fighters enter for their own ridiculous reasons.

Some want revenge.  
Some want respect.  
Some want money.  
Some want property back.  
Some misunderstood the flyer.  
Some just heard there would be free hot dogs.

At first, the tournament feels like trashy fun.

Then it gets bigger.

Then it gets uglier.

Tripp rigs matchups, exaggerates backstories, invents rivalries, creates fake drama, humiliates contestants, dodges questions about the prize money, sells bootleg merch, and turns everyone’s worst personality trait into a thumbnail.

Tripp thinks the fighters are dumb losers. He thinks they are beneath him. He thinks they should be grateful to be on his stream.

He is wrong.

By the end of the tournament, the fighters realize they are being played.

The final winner reaches the end of the bracket expecting the prize.

Instead, Tripp reveals a hidden clause: to claim victory, the champion must face the tournament host himself.

Tripp becomes the hidden final boss.

One boss.  
One final fight.  
One livestream arena full of drones, ring lights, fake sponsors, collapsing equipment, and a subscriber count dropping in real time.

The final battle is not just about winning the tournament.

It is about beating the man who thought everybody else was the joke.

## 3. Tone

The tone of **Sidewalk Showdown** should be:

- crude
- fast
- violent
- funny
- raunchy
- chaotic
- adult
- petty
- grimy
- weirdly heartfelt
- arcade-satirical
- mean in the moment, warm in the landing

The game should poke fun at everybody.

It makes fun of:

- influencers
- livestream audiences
- fake sponsors
- street legends
- local drunks
- wannabe cops
- conspiracy weirdos
- petty criminals
- scam artists
- bad business ideas
- neighborhood grudges
- people who think a parking lot is sacred land
- people who get into fights over absolutely nothing
- people who film fights instead of helping
- people who watch fights and pretend they are above it

Nobody is clean.

Nobody is normal.

Nobody gets out unroasted.

However, the game should not feel hollow, cruel, or emotionally empty. The fighters can be mocked, but they should also be memorable, specific, dangerous, funny, and strangely human.

The best tonal rule:

> Everyone can be mocked, but everyone should also be memorable.

The ending should be wholesome in a sideways way.

Not clean.  
Not sentimental.  
Not “everybody learns a lesson and opens a bakery.”

More like:

> A group of human disasters accidentally develops loyalty, exposes a worse human disaster, ruins his livestream empire, and walks away with slightly more respect for each other.

Then they immediately start arguing again.

## 4. Rating and Humor Direction

**Sidewalk Showdown** is intended as a **Mature-rated arcade comedy fighter**.

The story, dialogue, character banter, METHtalities, and worldbuilding may include:

- profanity
- crude jokes
- raunchy banter
- adult references
- drunken stupidity
- scam culture
- street violence
- gross-out humor
- black comedy
- bad decisions
- humiliating finishers
- morally questionable characters

The humor should feel nasty but not empty.

The game can be offensive, dirty, and mean, but it should be funny because the characters are specific, theatrical, and ridiculous — not because the world has no point of view.

The fighters can be the butt of jokes, but they should also get:

- wins
- comebacks
- punchlines
- reversals
- moments of strange dignity
- endings that make their personal nonsense matter

The game’s emotional landing should be:

> Everybody here is a disaster, but at least they are not that guy.

## 5. World

The game takes place in a fictional Florida-inspired arcade universe.

No real towns.  
No real brands.  
No real locations.  
No real public figures.

The setting should feel recognizable but exaggerated into myth.

This is not realistic Florida.

This is **Sidewalk Florida**.

A world where:

- every gas station has a legend
- every dumpster has politics
- every laundromat has rules
- every flea market booth might be illegal
- every parking lot has history
- every bar stool has emotional baggage
- every raccoon may be part of an organized structure
- every man with a selfie stick thinks he is a media empire

The world is hot, loud, overexposed, sunburned, sticky, neon-lit, and full of background characters who all look like they have beef with someone.

The stages are not just arenas.

They are local battlegrounds.

### World Keywords

- sunbaked

- sticky
- neon
- sweaty
- overexposed
- weirdly ceremonial
- trashy but colorful
- lawless but full of local rules
- ugly, funny, and alive

## 6. Main Antagonist

# Tripp Vlogs

**Role:** Main antagonist, tournament creator, livestream host, manipulator, commentator, scam artist, and hidden final boss.

**Important Canon Rule:** Tripp is **not playable**.

He is not part of the regular roster.

He is not a selectable fighter in arcade mode.

He is not one of many bosses.

He is the only boss in story/campaign mode, revealed at the very end after the player wins the tournament bracket.

---

### 6.1 Who Tripp Is

Tripp Vlogs is a washed-up content creator whose fame has rotted into desperation.

He used to have a following. Not a massive one, but enough to make him think he was special.

Then the views dried up.

His fans moved on.

His sponsors stopped answering emails.

His uploads became sadder, louder, and more pathetic.

Tripp cannot handle being ordinary. He believes attention is proof of value. If people are watching him, he matters. If they stop watching, he might have to become a real person.

That terrifies him.

Then the bus stop fight goes viral, and Tripp sees a way back.

He does not see human beings.

He sees content.

---

### 6.2 Public Persona

In public, Tripp acts like he is helping.

He calls **Sidewalk Showdown**:

- “a platform”
- “a movement”
- “a community event”
- “raw human storytelling”
- “combat-based outreach”
- “the future of local sports entertainment”

He pretends the tournament is about giving people a chance.

He talks like every fight is inspirational.

He frames exploitation as opportunity.

Possible Tripp lines:

> “I’m not using these people. I’m elevating them through conflict.”

> “This is bigger than violence. This is branding.”

> “Everybody here had a story. I just made it profitable.”

> “Some people call this exploitation. I call it vertical-format hope.”

> “They were fighting for free before I got here.”

---

### 6.3 Private Truth

Privately, Tripp thinks the fighters are beneath him.

He is not jealous of them.

He does not secretly admire them.

He does not think they are more interesting than him.

He thinks they are dumb losers, street bums, drunks, weirdos, criminals, freaks, nobodies, and walking punchlines.

In his mind, he is the genius.

He is the famous one.

He is the brand.

He is the reason anyone cares.

The fighters are just raw material.

He believes they should be grateful he pointed a camera at them.

Tripp does not care who gets hurt, humiliated, lied to, or destroyed, as long as the numbers go up.

If someone gets embarrassed, that is content.  
If someone gets angry, that is engagement.  
If someone gets injured, that is a cliffhanger.  
If someone breaks down emotionally, that is a thumbnail.

His cruelty comes from arrogance.

He believes fame makes him better than them.

He believes having followers makes him smarter than them.

He believes the people without platforms are too stupid to understand how badly they are being used.

That is his fatal mistake.

The fighters are messy, ridiculous, gross, selfish, and unstable.

But they are not stupid.

They know when they are being played.

And when they finally compare notes, Tripp’s whole scam starts falling apart.

---

### 6.4 Tripp’s Motivation

Tripp wants his fame back.

But more than that, he wants proof that he is above everyone else.

He wants the world to confirm what he already believes:

- he is the visionary
- he is the genius
- he is the brand
- everyone else is lucky to be near him

**Sidewalk Showdown** gives him power over people he already looks down on.

He controls the bracket.

He controls the edits.

He controls the camera.

He controls the story.

Or at least he thinks he does.

---

### 6.5 Tripp’s Tactics

Tripp manipulates the tournament by using:

- rigged matchups
- fake rivalries
- humiliating interviews
- bad-faith editing
- misleading contracts
- fake prize details
- sponsor stunts
- donation-based hazards
- live chat interference
- fake medical clearances
- unpaid interns
- emotional manipulation
- bootleg merchandise
- sob-story exaggeration
- villain edits
- public shaming
- surprise rule changes

He makes every fighter look worse than they are.

If someone is angry, he makes them look dangerous.

If someone is sad, he makes them look pathetic.

If someone is proud, he makes them look delusional.

If someone is funny, he steals the joke.

If someone pushes back, he calls them ungrateful.

---

### 6.6 Hidden Boss Reveal

After the player defeats the final tournament opponent, Tripp refuses to award the prize.

He stalls.

He checks fake paperwork.

He claims there are “engagement irregularities.”

Then he reveals the hidden clause.

The winner must defeat the tournament commissioner in a final livestream event to claim the prize.

This is the reveal:

> **Tripp Vlogs is the hidden boss.**

The arena transforms into **Tripp’s Livestream Arena**.

The crowd realizes the whole thing was rigged.

The fighters realize Tripp never intended to pay out honestly.

Tripp stops pretending to be generous.

He finally says what he really thinks.

Possible reveal line:

> “You people should be thanking me. Before me, you were just bums yelling in parking lots. I made you characters.”

That is the moment the final fight becomes personal.

---

### 6.7 Final Boss Fight

Tripp fights with technology, cheap spectacle, and influencer cowardice.

He is not a trained fighter.

He is a scammer with gear.

Possible attacks:

- **Selfie Stick Sweep** — long-range strikes
- **Ring Light Flash** — blinds/stuns the player
- **Drone Drop** — drones crash into the arena
- **Comment Section Barrage** — toxic chat messages become projectiles
- **Sponsor Chug** — drinks a shady energy drink for a temporary buff
- **Apology Armor** — fake-cries to gain defense
- **Thumbnail Trap** — giant red arrows and captions clutter the screen
- **Algorithm Shift** — changes arena hazards mid-fight
- **Cancel Panic** — desperate final phase where he attacks faster as his follower count drops

The boss fight should feel like the player is not just fighting Tripp.

They are fighting his whole content machine.

---

### 6.8 Tripp’s Downfall

Tripp loses because he underestimates everyone.

He thought the fighters were too dumb to notice the scam.

He thought the crowd was too stupid to turn on him.

He thought the stream belonged to him.

He thought being watched meant being untouchable.

After his defeat, his own livestream exposes him.

His raw footage leaks.

His fake contracts are shown.

His sponsors abandon him.

His audience turns the comments against him.

His apology video auto-uploads while he is still insulting everyone.

His final punishment is not death.

It is humiliation.

Demonetization.

Irrelevance.

And worst of all:

Everybody else becomes more famous than him.

Their fights should be petty, personal, and weirdly inspiring.

---

---

### Everyone vs. Tripp

At first, the fighters tolerate Tripp because they want the prize.

Then they hate him because he lies.

Then they unite against him because he assumes they are too stupid to fight back.

This should be the emotional turn of the story.

Not “the fighters become noble heroes.”

More like:

## 10. METHtality System

Each playable fighter has a cinematic signature finishing move called a **METHtality**.

Because the game is Mature-rated, **METHtality** is intentionally crude, dirty, offensive, bootleg, and adult from the jump.

It is memorable.

It is grimy.

It fits the trashy arcade parody tone.

It should be treated as an in-world term that Tripp promotes because he thinks it is edgy and marketable.

The characters can make fun of the name.

Example:

> Peggy: “That name sucks.”  
> Tripp: “It tested great with degenerates.”  
> Gretchen: “You tested it in a gas station bathroom.”  
> Tripp: “That is a focus group.”

### METHtality Rules

A METHtality should be:

- character-specific
- visually memorable
- funny
- over-the-top
- fast enough for arcade pacing
- crude but readable
- absurd rather than realistic
- built around the fighter’s personal gimmick

A METHtality should not be generic.

The best METHtalities should feel like:

> “Only this character would do this, and only in this game would it make sense.”

## 11. Campaign Structure

The campaign should feel like a tournament, but it does not need to be a mathematically perfect single-elimination bracket.

In-world, Tripp calls it a bracket, but the structure is sketchy because he changes the rules whenever it benefits the stream.

This supports the story.

### Recommended Campaign Route Per Fighter

Each playable fighter campaign should follow this structure:

1. **Intro Scene** — the fighter’s reason for entering.
2. **Fight 1: Early Local Opponent** — establishes tone and basic tournament stakes.
3. **Fight 2: Personality Clash** — funny matchup based on contrasting worldviews.
4. **Fight 3: Rival Fight** — character-specific grudge match.
5. **Fight 4: Sponsor/Stunt Fight** — Tripp adds fake sponsors or weird conditions.
6. **Fight 5: Late-Bracket Chaos Fight** — the scam begins cracking open.
7. **Fight 6: Final Tournament Opponent** — the fighter wins the bracket.
8. **Hidden Boss: Tripp Vlogs** — final fight in Tripp’s Livestream Arena.
9. **Character Ending** — personal payoff plus Tripp’s public humiliation.

### Campaign Escalation

```text
The Clip
↓
Tournament Announcement
↓
Early Bracket
↓
The Stream Gets Bigger
↓
The Scam Cracks Open
↓
Late-Game Chaos
↓
Hidden Boss Reveal
↓
Tripp’s Livestream Arena
↓
Character Ending
```

---

### 11.1 Opening Cinematic: “The Clip”

The game opens at **The Bus Stop**.

Tripp is filming a desperate livestream challenge. Something embarrassing. Something nobody asked for.

Behind him, two locals start arguing.

The fight begins over something stupid:

- stolen shade
- a moved milk crate
- a wet grocery bag
- someone sitting on “the wrong part” of the bench
- a raccoon-related misunderstanding

Tripp turns around.

The camera zooms in.

The fight goes viral overnight.

Tripp wakes up famous again.

The next day, he prints a bracket.

---

### 11.2 Act I: The Tournament Announcement

Tripp announces **Sidewalk Showdown** from a folding table in a parking lot.

The flyer promises:

- cash
- fame
- housing
- redemption
- a fresh start
- sponsor opportunities
- “official sidewalk champion status”

Nobody fully understands the rules.

Nobody fully trusts Tripp.

Everybody still signs up.

Because every fighter has something to prove.

---

### 11.3 Act II: Early Bracket

Early fights are small, funny, and personal.

Possible early stages:

- The Bus Stop
- The Liquor Store
- The Parking Lot
- The Laundromat
- Gas-N-Go

Tripp introduces the fighters with exaggerated backstories, insulting nicknames, and false emotional angles.

The fighters begin to clash over old grudges and new lies.

The tournament feels like a trashy local event that accidentally became entertaining.

---

### 11.4 Act III: The Stream Gets Bigger

The tournament starts pulling real numbers.

Tripp upgrades the production.

Now there are:

- drones
- sponsor banners
- fake merch booths
- live polls
- donation goals
- punishment rounds
- weird crowd chants
- “exclusive confessionals”
- mid-fight advertisements

The fights become more ridiculous and more dangerous.

Tripp starts pushing fighters harder because the audience likes conflict.

The fighters begin to realize he is not just recording drama.

He is creating it.

---

### 11.5 Act IV: The Scam Cracks Open

The fighters begin comparing what Tripp told each of them.

The prize details do not match.

The contracts contradict each other.

The sponsor money is missing.

The housing offer is fake.

The “fresh start” is actually a makeover series.

The cash prize is buried under conditions, fees, taxes, deductions, and “brand participation requirements.”

The fighters realize Tripp has been lying to all of them.

Not just a little.

The whole tournament is a con.

---

### 11.6 Act V: Late-Game Chaos

The tournament continues because the fighters still want to win.

But now they are fighting for more than the prize.

They want payback.

They start hijacking Tripp’s production.

They interrupt ads.

They ruin sponsor reads.

They expose raw footage.

They use his cameras against him.

They turn his livestream into evidence.

Tripp gets meaner, nastier, and more desperate.

Possible late-game stages:

- Dumpster Alley
- The Drainage Canal
- The Flea Market
- The Alley Behind the Bar

By the end of this act, Tripp is barely controlling the show.

The fighters are still idiots.

But now they are organized idiots.

That is much worse for him.

---

### 11.7 Act VI: Hidden Boss

The player wins the bracket.

Tripp refuses to pay.

He reveals the hidden final clause.

The final stage loads:

> **Tripp’s Livestream Arena**

Tripp steps into the arena wearing ridiculous influencer combat gear.

The ring lights flare.

The drones activate.

The fake crowd noise plays.

The subscriber counter starts glitching.

The final fight begins.

## 12. Ending Direction

After Tripp is beaten, the livestream collapses into total humiliation.

The fighters expose him.

The audience turns on him.

His fake sponsors disappear.

His apology video destroys him further.

The winner gets their ending.

The other fighters get small signs of victory too.

The tournament ends with a strange sense of community.

Nobody becomes perfect.

Nobody suddenly becomes normal.

But they all know one thing:

Tripp thought they were disposable.

He was wrong.

Then someone immediately steals the trophy, and everybody starts arguing again.

Cut to credits.

### Ending Feeling

The final emotional note should be:

> “Everybody here is a disaster, but at least they are not that guy.”

## 13. Canon Rules

These rules define what is currently true for the project.

### 13.1 Story Canon

- Tripp Vlogs is the main antagonist.
- Tripp Vlogs is not playable.
- Tripp Vlogs is the only boss.
- Tripp is hidden until the end of story/campaign mode.
- The player must win the tournament bracket before fighting Tripp.
- Sidewalk Showdown is presented as a tournament but is structurally sketchy because Tripp manipulates it.
- The tournament is a livestreamed scam disguised as opportunity.
- The prize package is intentionally vague, manipulative, and full of conditions.
- Tripp controls the edit, but not the truth.
- The fighters eventually realize they are being exploited.
- The ending is weirdly wholesome, but not clean or sentimental.

### 13.2 Tone Canon

- The game is Mature-rated.
- The humor is raunchy, crude, adult, and chaotic.
- The game pokes fun at everybody.
- Nobody is fully innocent.
- Nobody is fully normal.
- Every fighter should be roast-worthy and memorable.
- The comedy should be character-specific, not generic.
- The tone should be mean in the moment but warm in the landing.
- The game should feel like a playable urban legend.

### 13.3 World Canon

- The setting is fictional.
- Use no real towns as stages.
- Use no real brands.
- Use no real public figures.
- The world is Sidewalk Florida, not realistic Florida.
- Stages are recognizable place types, not actual locations.
- Every stage should contain local history, weird background gags, and implied grudges.

### 13.4 Roster Canon

- The playable roster currently has 15 fighters.
- Tripp Vlogs is not part of the playable roster.
- Every fighter needs a public reason and private reason for entering.
- Every fighter needs a rivalry.
- Every fighter needs a character-specific METHtality.
- Every fighter gets a unique ending.
- Every fighter should be funny, flawed, and agency-driven.

### 13.5 METHtality Canon

- METHtality is the official finisher system name.
- It is intentionally crude and adult.
- It exists inside the world as a branded tournament feature.
- Tripp likely coined or promoted the term.
- Fighters may mock the name in dialogue.
- Every playable fighter has one unique METHtality.
- METHtalities should be absurd, funny, and character-specific.

### 13.6 Campaign Canon

- Campaign mode is character-based.
- Each selected fighter experiences the tournament through their own warped worldview.
- Tripp manipulates every fighter differently.
- The route should include early fights, rival fights, sponsor/stunt escalation, scam reveal, late-game chaos, hidden boss, and ending.
- The final fight is always against Tripp.
- Tripp’s Livestream Arena is always the final stage.

### 13.7 Ending Canon

- Tripp loses publicly.
- His livestream exposes him.
- His sponsors abandon him.
- His apology video makes everything worse.
- The fighters reclaim the tournament.
- The winner gets a personal ending.
- The other fighters get small signs of victory.
- The group does not become noble heroes.
- They become slightly more loyal disasters.
- The final mood is triumphant, filthy, funny, and weirdly wholesome.

## 14. Final Story Statement

**Sidewalk Showdown** is a raunchy arcade fighting comedy about a cruel livestreamer who builds a tournament out of people he thinks are beneath him.

Tripp Vlogs sees the fighters as bums, freaks, losers, drunks, weirdos, and disposable content.

He thinks fame makes him better.

He thinks a camera makes him powerful.

He thinks everyone else is too stupid to notice the scam.

But the fighters are not just punchlines.

They are punchlines with agency.

They are ridiculous, messy, foul-mouthed, violent, petty, and half-insane.

They are also tougher, smarter, and more loyal than Tripp expects.

By the end, they do not become perfect people.

They become something better for this world:

A bunch of absolute disasters who finally agree on one thing.

Tripp has to go down.

And when he does, the sidewalk wins.
